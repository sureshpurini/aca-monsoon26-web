Why step 3 buys nothing — open only after you have predicted

Between steps 2 and 3, L2, L3 and DRAM traffic all fall. The bound does not move at all, because L1 traffic never changed, and 8 GB at 8.192 TB/s bounds you at 1024 GF/s no matter what happens outside it. Raising a hit rate reduces traffic at levels further out and leaves the level itself untouched. Only step 4 — cutting the requests L1 ever sees — moves the L1 ceiling. Hit rate and traffic are different levers.

And note where step 4 lands: 97.5% of peak, with DRAM binding again. It never becomes compute-bound. You are never "done", only bound somewhere cheaper.

Panel 2 — The area split

Area buys the two axes on different curves: π = α·Aalu is linear; I = κ√Amem is a square root — and it saturates, because a fixed problem has only so much reuse in it. β is held fixed because the memory interface is reserved.

P = min( α(A − m) ,   r · min(√m, scap) )     r = βκ/α    A = α = 1

The two limits, and the catch in the first one

β scarce (r → 0): m → A — spend everything on SRAM. β plentiful (r large): m ≈ A²/r² — spend it on ALUs. One formula, both design philosophies.

But drag r towards zero and watch the performance readout. It goes to zero too, and at β = 0 every split scores zero and there is no unique optimum at all. "Spend everything on cache" is a bad prescription dressed as an insight.

And turn the cap down. Past saturation more SRAM buys nothing, a whole range of splits ties, and the optimum stops sitting at compute–memory equality. Under scarce bandwidth a finite workload need not push memory area toward the whole die.
